Here Today, Gone Tomorrow: How Software as a Service Makes Software Preservation a Nightmare.

Vaminta
07/11/2025
FluffOpinion

Obtaining software couldn’t be easier. Long gone are the days of inserting multiple floppy disks, one after the other to install Windows. These days, the only physical copies of windows are the ones that let you look outside. Granted, if you go to great efforts you can find yourself a physical copy of Windows 11, but with online sign-in mandatory, is that really such a liberty anymore? Even if you keep your copy of Windows 11 safe from the physical elements, there’s always the real possibility that signing in will one day be impossible, and when that happens, you’ll never be able to access the desktop from a fresh install.

Since the late 2010s, software companies have been flocking to the subscription model of selling software. Selling digital services as a subscription isn’t exactly new technology however. From as early as 2009 Spotify brought the idea of paying monthly for a seemingly unlimited catalogue of music to thousands of users. By the end of 2011 that number grew to two million. Netflix is another big name who brought the idea of paying monthly to watch movies to the masses. Naturally, the success of such a business model can catch the attention of others. Who doesn’t want a continuous cashflow coming in every month?

The new ability to pay monthly seems innocuous at first, few people are bothered by new options, however, the uprising of such a profitable model came at the sacrifice of owning anything. Why sell your product once, when you can sell it every month and change the price at any time. Instead of trying to sell upgrades, you can force new features on everyone and charge them for the privilege.

Adobe, the maker of photoshop and other graphics software, was very quick to change its business model. Instead of paying hundreds of dollars for a perpetual license, you’ll now need to fork out up to 70 dollars per month to rent their software. After 12 months you’re going to find yourself forking out more money than you ever did to own a copy in the past. Adobe gets to see a nice consistent cashflow to show to investors, and you get to rent their software and pray they don’t change the terms of the deal by increasing the price, changing features or completely retiring the product. Software as a Service takes the power from the user and gives it to the seller.

This also raises challenges for software preservation. Software as a service requires internet access to check that you have paid to access the software. Connections are made to proprietary servers to check ownership, which are hosted by the company. Even software which is sold as a "perpetual license” can be taken away at any time. Some software, including games cannot even run at all without an active internet connection. This unpleasant fact was brought to the attention of everyone who “bought” a copy of “The Crew”, a driving game released by Ubisoft, a large game developer, in 2014. After March, 2024, anyone who had bought a copy of the game would no longer be able to play it, as servers required for play, shutdown, leaving players with an expensive Blu-ray mug coaster. It was never made clear that the game could become unplayable after 10 years.

There are multiple channels on YouTube which specialise in visiting old, odd, and sometimes forgotten software. Many of them attract hundreds of thousands of views per video. Software preservation is clearly of great interest to many people, however, with modern software becoming increasingly difficult to preserve, what will people of the future know about the software technology of today?
Microsoft Publisher, a staple of the massively successful Microsoft Office suite, is being discontinued in 2026 at the ripe old age of 35. While sad, it’s understandable, many people have moved onto other products and Microsoft would rather you use its alternatives. You better hope you have an old perpetual license of the product though, because if you’re paying for Office 365, you’ll suddenly lose the ability to open any publisher files you may have saved on your PC. As per Microsoft's word: Support for the perpetual version of Publisher will end on October 1, 2026, when Office LTSC 2021 reaches end of support. Microsoft 365 subscribers will not be able to access Publisher from that date forward. Therefore, it’s recommended that you publish your files to PDF, or another format of your choosing. PDF is great if the work is final, but if you want to make future changes, saving as another format might be better. It’s not ideal however as the document can lose features added to the document that aren’t supported when exporting to the different format.

The unfortunate reality is that software which is around today, may not be around next year, next month, or even tomorrow. Without legislation or a change in business and consumer spending, things are unlikely to change. Software has become increasingly perishable. It’s a real possibility that the game you bought last week could be outlived by that tin of beans, or packet of pasta you have in your kitchen cupboard.

Unless you’re an executive at one of the aforementioned businesses or plethora of others, you’re unlikely to be able to convince them to change. However, there are options. Free and open source software is gaining the attention of more and more people. Its premise is simple, you are free to do with it as you please. Want to use it straight out of the box? Go ahead! Want to modify a part which bothers you? Sure, if you have the ability! Want to install it on every computer in your house? Nobody is going to stop you. Free software not only costs nothing, but more importantly, it gives you the freedom to do what you want with your copy. There is a fantastic library of Free Open Software out there, you just have to search a little. There’s rarely any marketing funds for free software so you must find it yourself. Although here’s a brief list of some of the best:

  • Linux - Free and open source operating system. Comes in various flavours.
  • Firefox - Open source web browser which supports the open web.
  • Libre Office - A comprehensive office suite, much like Microsoft Office.
  • GIMP - An image manipulation program.
  • KDen Live - A powerful video editor.
  • Blender - Powerful 3D modelling software.
  • (and countless tools for developers)

New legislation could help solve these issues, however this doesn’t seem likely to happen soon in the UK or USA. Following the closure of the aforementioned “The Crew” game, a petition was launched on the UK Government’s official petitions site. The petition ended up garnering 189,888 signatures from UK residents, throwing it well above the 10,000 threshold required for parliament to respond. The response was rather simple: “There are no plans to amend UK consumer law on disabling video games.” However, the number of signatures passed the 100,000 threshold required for a parliamentary debate. This is currently scheduled for November third, and you can watch it live on the “UK Parliament” YouTube Channel. While the prospect of new legislation seems slim, there are still actions the individual can take.

Louis Rossman, a business owner who also runs a YouTube channel advocating for better consumer rights has amassed 2.45 million subscribers, potentially showing there is a growing interest for updated legislation to help tackle issues such as the lack of ownership - a feature of SaaS. In the Summer of 2025 he founded a consumer rights wiki with the goal of helping inform consumers of anti-consumer actions particular companies have done. The hope being that consumers can stay away from businesses which implement anti consumer tactics. The scope is far broader but the sentiment is the same, people are fed up with the current direction technology is heading and the increase of Software as a Service only helps ensure that you own nothing that you use.

Software as a service can have its benefits, such as quick access and lower entry fees, however the drawbacks far outweigh any benefits the consumer gets.
